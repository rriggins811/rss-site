The listing presentation is a beautiful piece of work. It really is.

Somebody sits at your mother's kitchen table with a folder, a tablet, and a stack of comparable sales. There is a page about their marketing plan. There is a page with their photo on a bench. There is a number at the end, circled, that is a little higher than the last agent's number.

And you walk out of that meeting thinking you just learned something about how well that person sells houses.

You did not. You learned how well that person sells.

Those are two completely different jobs, and the gap between them is where families get hurt.

## A presentation measures the wrong skill

I spent years on the buying side of real estate. I mailed letters at paid-off houses owned by people in their seventies and eighties, then sat at their kitchen tables. I got good at that conversation. I know exactly what a polished pitch is measuring, because I built one.

A pitch measures comfort under a stranger's gaze. It measures rehearsal. It measures whether somebody can hold a room for half an hour and leave you feeling calm.

Selling your parents' house measures something else entirely. It measures whether an agent knows what happens when the buyer's inspector opens a panel in an old Fisher Park house and does not like what he sees. Whether they have sat with two siblings who want opposite things and kept the deal alive anyway. Whether they know how to price a house that has not been updated in decades without insulting the family who lives in it.

You cannot see any of that in a presentation. So you have to ask.

## The pressure is structural, and it is not personal

Here is the part most people in my industry will not say plainly.

An agent gets paid when a house sells. Not when a house is priced well. Not when a family makes the right call. When it sells.

That means every conversation, no matter how kind the agent is, has a gentle slope in it, and the slope runs toward a signed listing agreement. Toward listing now instead of in April. Toward selling instead of renting it out for two years. Toward selling instead of not selling at all.

I am not saying agents are dishonest. Most of the ones I know in Guilford County are good people who work hard and genuinely care about their clients. I am saying the incentive exists whether anyone acknowledges it or not, and a good agent knows it is there.

The agents I trust most are the ones who say it out loud before I do.

That is also why timing gets compressed. Families who are already exhausted meet an agent who is already leaning toward action, and three weeks later the house is on the market with nobody having asked whether this was the right season to sell. I wrote about that pattern in [the 90 day mistake](/blog/the-90-day-mistake-why-most-families-sell-their-parents-home-under-duress), because it is the single most common way good families end up with a bad outcome.

## Designations are a starting point, not a qualification

You will see letters after agents' names. SRES is the one you will run into most, the Seniors Real Estate Specialist designation, earned through coursework offered through the National Association of Realtors.

I am not knocking it. An agent who went and took that class is telling you they care about this work. That matters.

But a designation is a class somebody took. Experience is a hundred hard phone calls somebody made. They are not the same thing, and the letters on a business card cannot tell them apart.

The test is simple. Ask the agent with the designation to tell you about the last three senior or estate transactions they closed. If the answer has real texture in it, names of problems, timelines that slipped, a cleanout that went sideways, you are talking to someone who has done the work. If the answer stays general and stays smooth, you are still in the presentation.

## The questions I would ask, in the order I would ask them

Print these. Take them to every interview. Ask the same ones of every agent so you can actually compare the answers.

### How many estate or senior transition sales have you closed?

Not how many houses. How many sales where the owner had died, or moved into memory care, or moved in with a daughter in Winston-Salem, or was selling under a power of attorney.

Listen for whether they answer with a number and then keep talking. Experienced agents cannot help themselves. They start telling you about the one in Sedgefield with the flooded crawlspace.

### What happens if we need to slow down?

This is the most important question on the list and almost nobody asks it.

Say the house goes on the market and then your dad has a fall, or the family fractures, or you discover the estate does not have clear authority to sell yet. What does this agent do?

The answer you want is some version of "we pause, and here is exactly how." The answer you do not want is a long pause followed by talk about the listing agreement term.

### Who handles the cleanout question?

Sixty years of a life live in that house. Somebody has to deal with it, and the family is usually the least equipped to do it while grieving.

Ask who the agent uses. Ask whether they have estate sale people, junk haulers, and donation contacts they actually work with, or whether they will hand you a Google search. Ask whether they have ever told a family to sell a house as is and skip the cleanout entirely, because sometimes that is the right math and sometimes it is not.

### Will you tell us not to sell if that is the right answer?

Ask it exactly like that. Then be quiet and let them answer.

You are handing them permission to talk themselves out of a paycheck. What they do with that permission is the single most useful thing you will learn all day.

Good answers sound like: "Yes, and here is a time I did it." Or: "Yes, though I will tell you honestly that I want the listing, so get a second opinion on anything that big."

### Who else needs to be at this table before we list?

A strong agent for this kind of sale will name people who are not them. An elder law or estate attorney. A CPA, because the tax picture on an inherited or long-held house is not something an agent should be guessing at. A care coordinator if a parent's living situation is still unsettled.

An agent who thinks they are the whole answer is not the whole answer.

### How do you price a house that has not been updated in thirty years?

You want to hear a method, not a number. Anybody can pull comparable sales. Ask what they do with a house that is structurally sound but cosmetically dated, and whether they recommend pre-listing repairs and why or why not.

If the first agent's number is dramatically higher than everybody else's, that is not good news. That is a bid for the listing.

## Answers that mean you should keep looking

A few things I would walk away from.

Pressure on the calendar with no reason attached to it. "The market is about to shift" is not a reason. Ask what specifically they think is going to change and by when.

Vagueness about their fee, or discomfort when you ask how they get paid. Every professional in this should be able to answer that in one clean sentence, including me.

Any suggestion that your family should skip the attorney. Authority to sell a house on behalf of a living parent or a deceased one is a legal question, not a real estate question, and the answer depends on your documents and your situation. Ask an elder law or estate attorney. Anyone 60 or older can reach Legal Aid of North Carolina's Senior Law Project free of charge at 1-877-579-7562.

An agent who mentions a cash buyer they know personally, or offers to buy the house themselves, without clearly separating those two roles for you. That is a different transaction with a different set of incentives, and I have written plainly about [what the cash offer actually costs a family](/blog/we-buy-houses-aging-parents-cash-offer-cost).

## The order to do this in

Most families do this backwards. They interview agents first, and every decision after that gets shaped by whoever was most persuasive at the kitchen table.

Here is the better order.

**First, settle the authority question.** Who legally has the right to sell this house, and is that documented right now? If a parent is living, this usually turns on a power of attorney, and whether a particular document actually grants the power to sell real estate depends on how that document was written. If a parent has died, it usually runs through the estate, and what that takes depends on the will, the deed, and how the property was titled. Do not guess at any of it. I am not an attorney. Ask an elder law or estate attorney. Anyone 60 or older can also get free legal help through Legal Aid of North Carolina's Senior Law Project at 1-877-579-7562.

**Second, decide whether selling is even the right move this year.** Housing, care costs, taxes, and family logistics all sit in this decision, and none of them get clearer once a sign is in the yard.

**Third, get a rough sense of the money before anyone pitches you.** Knowing roughly what would be left after the sale changes how you hear every presentation. Run your own numbers with the [net proceeds calculator](/tools/net-proceeds-calculator) so nobody has to hand you a figure you cannot check.

**Fourth, then interview agents.** Two or three, same questions, written answers where you can get them.

**Fifth, choose based on the hard answers, not the warm ones.** Warmth is real and it matters. It is just not the thing that saves you money or saves your family from a bad ninety days.

## One last thing

The hardest part of this is that the person sitting across your mother's kitchen table is usually likable. That is not an accident and it is not a trick. Likable people do well in my industry.

But your family is about to hand somebody the largest asset your parents ever owned, during the worst season of your life, on the basis of a thirty minute conversation. You are allowed to make that conversation harder. You are allowed to ask questions that create a little silence.

The right agent will not mind. In my experience they will be relieved, because the families who ask hard questions up front are the families who do not fall apart in week six.

Ask them anyway. Even of me.
