You know how it goes once Mom moves into assisted living. The house just sits there. It's paid off, the lights are on a timer, and you drive by when you can, which isn't often, because you live two states away. It feels like the safest thing your family owns. Nobody's in it, so nothing can happen to it.

That's exactly what makes it a target.

I spent eight years on the buying side of real estate before I walked away from it. I know how easy it is to find an empty, paid-off house whose owner gets the tax bill at a different address, because that's the list a lot of investors build on purpose. The difference is that an investor sends a letter. The people in this story don't bother asking. They pretend to be your mother, hire a real agent, and sell the house to a real buyer while you're at work.

This isn't a scare piece. It's one of the most preventable frauds out there, and almost every protection is free. It takes an afternoon, and you can do most of it from your couch tonight.

If you read our earlier post on forged powers of attorney after a parent dies, this is its cousin. That one is about a fake document after a death. This one is about a fake person while your parent is still alive, living somewhere safe, and has no idea anyone's using their name.

## What Federal Prosecutors Said Last Week

On October 20, 2026, the US Attorney's Office for the District of Massachusetts announced that a 61-year-old man from Carrollton, Texas, agreed to plead guilty to wire fraud conspiracy and money laundering conspiracy. He was charged with two co-defendants in July 2026. These are allegations, and every defendant is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

Here's what the court filings allege, per the US Attorney's Office. The group looked for real properties that were vacant and unencumbered (meaning no mortgage) in Massachusetts, Georgia, Indiana and Tennessee, owned by people who lived out of state. They set up email and internet phone accounts in the owners' names and got fake driver's licenses and passports. Then they posed as the owners, got real estate professionals to list and negotiate the sales, and had the properties deeded to buyers who had no idea anything was wrong. Since about June 2023, prosecutors say, the group shared or tried to share about $1.5 million.

Read that list again: vacant, no mortgage, owner lives somewhere else. That describes a lot of houses that belong to parents in assisted living or memory care.

It isn't one case, either. In June 2026 the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) issued a public warning about criminals impersonating owners of vacant property to sell it without the owner's knowledge or consent. The FBI says they pull owner information from county and state public websites, data brokers, stolen accounts and phishing, then contact a local agent and a title company and draft the sale contracts. Proceeds go to a co-conspirator in another state.

And North Carolina's own regulator is watching it. In its October 2026 eBulletin, the North Carolina Real Estate Commission said seller impersonation fraud continues to affect transactions, particularly vacant land, inherited property, abandoned homes and remotely owned property, and that scammers now use forged ID, fake notarizations and even AI-generated video. The Commission has also disciplined brokers who listed properties without making sure the person asking was the real owner.

The money is real. According to the FBI's 2025 IC3 annual report, people 60 and older filed 2,473 real estate fraud complaints in 2025 and reported $123,671,936 in losses, up from $76,324,236 the year before. That category is broader than stolen houses (it also covers rental and timeshare fraud), so it isn't a count of homes sold out from under owners. It's a floor, not a ceiling. FBI Boston has said reported losses are most likely much higher because many people don't know where to report or haven't realized they've been scammed.

## What This Means for Your Family

### Your parent's house checks every box

You don't have to do anything wrong for this to happen. Your parent paid off the mortgage, which is a good thing. Your parent moved somewhere safer, which is a good thing. You live where your job is, which is normal. Put those three good decisions together and you get the exact profile the FBI and the Real Estate Commission describe: an empty, paid-off house with an owner who can't easily be reached.

FBI Boston put it plainly in a 2025 warning: scammers comb public records for vacant properties with no mortgage or lien, impersonate the owner, and ask an agent to list it. Owners often don't know until someone alerts them, sometimes after the sale has already gone through.

### The system isn't built to catch a fake seller for you

You'd think the county would stop a fake deed. Guilford County's Register of Deeds says otherwise: under state law, it must accept and record any document that meets the legal requirements and fees, and it isn't responsible for verifying whether the document is valid or authentic. The same page lists the elderly and owners of vacant property among the people most targeted.

Agents are supposed to verify sellers, and good ones do. But the Real Estate Commission's October bulletin makes a point worth sitting with: a seller's name that "matched the tax records" proves very little, because a scammer can read the same public records you can. The fake seller in these schemes isn't guessing. They know your mother's full name, the parcel number, and the tax value. What they don't have is anything only your family would have. That's your edge.

### What the fake seller looks like from the other side

The FBI and the Commission list the same warning signs, and it helps to know them because you're the one most likely to spot them. The "seller" only talks by email, text or an internet phone number. They won't meet in person or get on a live video call, and there's always a reason: travel, illness, a family emergency. They want a quick, all-cash closing, they're fine with a price below market, and they want money wired to an account in another name or another state. They don't know the house. Ask about the back porch that isn't there, and they'll happily agree it needs work.

Your mother would never act like that, because she'd want to talk to the agent, see the paperwork, and argue about the price. The protection is making sure the people in the middle of a sale can reach the real family fast.

### The first 72 hours matter

FBI Boston says it can work with partners to try to stop wire transfers and recover funds within the first 72 hours. That's the whole case for setting up alerts now. A fake sale caught on day one is a phone call and a frozen wire. A fake sale caught six months later is a lawsuit, an attorney, and a title mess that can take a long time to unwind.

## What to Do This Week

### 1. Set a listing alert on the address

FBI Boston's first tips for property owners include setting up online search alerts for the property. Go to two or three of the big home-listing websites, search your parent's exact address, and save it or turn on alerts for it. If a listing ever appears that your family didn't authorize, you'll know before a buyer's attorney schedules the closing. It takes ten minutes and costs nothing.

### 2. Sign up for your county's property fraud alert

The FBI recommends checking whether your county's Register of Deeds offers a notification service that emails or texts you when a document is recorded under your name. Guilford County offers one. Call your parent's county and ask if it does too. Sign up under your parent's full name, every variation of it (middle initial, no middle initial, maiden name, "Jr."), and the name of any trust that holds the house. Guilford is clear that the alert doesn't block a recording. It warns you after one is filed, which is exactly the 72-hour window you need.

### 3. Keep the tax bill and the water bill coming to someone who opens them

FBI Boston says to take action if the water or property tax bills stop arriving, or if utility bills on a vacant property suddenly go up. So make sure those bills reach a family member who'll actually read them. Call the county tax office and confirm the mailing address on the parcel. If the mail goes to the empty house, forward it to you. A bill that quietly stops coming is a smoke alarm, not a paperwork glitch.

### 4. Give a neighbor your phone number

The FBI suggests driving by the property or having someone check it, and asking neighbors to tell you if they see anything suspicious. A "For Sale" sign, a lockbox, a photographer, or a stranger walking the yard with a clipboard is something a neighbor notices in an hour and you might not notice for a month. One text to the family next door with your number and "please call me if anyone shows up to sell or show the house" covers you better than any app.

### 5. Find the owner's title insurance policy

The FBI's June 2026 warning says to review your title insurance coverage, because standard owner's policies may protect against forgery and may cover the legal costs of clearing the title. Your parent probably got one when they bought the house. Look in the closing folder from that purchase, or call the closing attorney's office or title company named on the old settlement papers. Put a copy with the deed and the will.

### 6. If you see something, move the same day

If a listing, a sign or a recorded document shows up that your family didn't authorize, call the listing brokerage that day, tell them you represent the owner, and ask them to stop. Then file at ic3.gov. The FBI asks for every phone number, email address, bank account and name involved. The Real Estate Commission also points to the North Carolina Attorney General. Call local law enforcement and a real estate attorney too. Keep screenshots of everything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is seller impersonation fraud?

It's when a scammer pretends to be a property's real owner and hires a real estate agent to sell it. The FBI says criminals use fake driver's licenses or passports, new email accounts and internet phone numbers built from public records, then route the sale money to an account they control. It usually targets vacant, paid-off property whose owner lives somewhere else.

### Can someone sell my parent's house without them knowing?

Someone can try, and some have succeeded. FBI Boston says owners sometimes don't learn their property was listed until after the sale went through. The best defense is early warning: a listing alert on the address, your county's deed fraud alert, and making sure the tax bill and mail reach a family member who reads them.

### Which houses do scammers target for fake sales?

The FBI and the North Carolina Real Estate Commission both point to vacant land, empty homes, inherited or estate property, and property whose owner lives out of state. FBI Boston adds that scammers look for properties with no mortgage or lien. A paid-off house sitting empty after a parent moves into care fits that description.

### Does title insurance protect against deed fraud?

It may. The FBI's June 2026 warning says standard owner's title insurance policies may provide protection against forgery and may cover the legal costs of restoring rightful ownership. Find the policy from when your parent bought the house and read what it covers, or ask the title company that issued it.

### What should I do if my parent's house is listed for sale without permission?

Act the same day. Call the listing brokerage and tell them the owner didn't authorize the sale, then file a complaint at ic3.gov with every name, phone number, email and bank account involved. Contact local police and a real estate attorney. FBI Boston says it can try to stop wire transfers within the first 72 hours.
