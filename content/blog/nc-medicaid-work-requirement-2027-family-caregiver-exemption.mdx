You know how the person who does the most for Mom is usually the one with the least to fall back on? I watched that for eight years from the wrong side of the kitchen table. I was the cash buyer, and the adult child sitting across from me was almost always the one who'd cut back to part time, or quit outright, to keep a parent at home. Not the sibling with the pension. The one whose own health insurance had quietly turned into NC Medicaid somewhere in the second year of caregiving, and who'd never said that out loud to anybody.

Here's why I'm writing this one now. On January 1, 2027, North Carolina starts a work requirement for most adults ages 19 through 64 who have Medicaid through expansion. Eighty hours a month of work, school or volunteering, or $580 a month of household income, with proof. Miss it and you can lose the coverage.

And here's the part that got buried under the headlines. The federal rule that created this requirement says, in plain words, that a family caregiver of a person with a disability is excluded from it. It goes further and says "disabled individual" can include an older adult who needs help bathing, dressing, eating or getting around. If that's your parent, you may not have to meet the 80 hours at all.

But nobody at the county is going to figure that out for you. You have to raise your hand, and you have to bring paper.

So this is the post I'd hand to a friend in that chair. Who the rule hits, who it doesn't, what the exemption says word for word, what it means for your parent's house, and the six things to do before the first letter shows up.

## What Changed, and When

The requirement comes from the federal budget law passed in 2025, the one you've seen called H.R. 1. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) issued the rule that carries it out, CMS-2454-IFC, on June 1, 2026. It was published in the Federal Register on June 3, 2026 (91 FR 33348), took effect July 31, 2026, and tells states they must implement the requirement no later than January 1, 2027.

Per the CMS fact sheet, it applies to non-pregnant adults between the ages of 19 and 64 who are not on Medicare and who are in the Medicaid adult group, which is the expansion group in North Carolina. To stay covered, you show for a month that you did one of these: worked at least 80 hours, completed at least 80 hours of community service, took part in a work program for 80 hours, enrolled at least half-time in school, combined those activities to reach 80 hours, or had monthly income at least equal to the federal minimum wage times 80 hours. CMS puts that income figure at $580 a month for 2026.

North Carolina's own page on the rule, updated October 13, 2026, adds the state's details:

- The rule starts January 1, 2027, for most adults ages 19 through 64 on NC Medicaid.
- You'll renew every 6 months instead of once a year.
- At renewal, you need to have met the requirement for only 3 of the 6 months before your recertification.
- If you're applying or reapplying, it's stricter: you must meet the requirement for 3 months in a row in the 3 months before you apply.
- If you lose coverage, you'll get a letter, and you usually have 60 days from the date on it to appeal. The NC Medicaid Ombudsman, 1-877-201-3750, is free and independent of your county DSS.

NC Health and Human Services Secretary Dev Sangvai said in the department's September 1, 2026 release that these federal changes "put hundreds of thousands of people at risk." That's the state's own words, not mine.

One more date that matters to the parent, not the caregiver. Per NC Medicaid, starting January 1, 2027, Medicaid will cover only 1 month of care before an application for most adults 19 through 64 without a disability, and 2 months for adults 65 and older or people with a disability. Applications filed by December 31, 2026 keep the old 3 months of retroactive coverage. If your parent may need Medicaid for long-term care soon, that's a reason to file this year, and I covered the rest of that decision in the October 9 post on the new proof rules.

## Your Parent Isn't the One This Hits. You Might Be.

Start with the relief. If your mother is 78 and on Medicaid for her care, this rule does not touch her. NC Medicaid's checklist excludes anyone older than 64, anyone who qualifies for Medicare, and anyone NC Medicaid has already determined has a disability. Her coverage continues the way it does today.

The person it reaches is the one reading this at 11 PM. The 52-year-old daughter who went to three days a week so somebody could drive Dad to dialysis. The 47-year-old son who moved back into the house after the second fall and hasn't had a W-2 since. If your own health coverage is NC Medicaid through expansion, you are the "applicable individual" in this rule, and the state will be looking at your hours, not your parent's.

That's the version of this I see most often, and it's the version nobody plans for. You didn't choose Medicaid as a strategy. It's just what was left after the caregiving ate the job.

## The Exemption, in the Rule's Own Words

The rule's exclusion list lives at 42 CFR 435.554. Number three on that list is "a parent, guardian, caretaker relative, or family caregiver" of a dependent child 13 or under or of a disabled individual. For family caregivers, the rule gives three ways to qualify, and you only need one:

1. You primarily live with the person and provide assistance "on a regular basis" that is "not solely incidental in nature."
2. You're a relative on the rule's list (it names son, daughter, stepson, stepdaughter, grandson, granddaughter, brother, sister, and more), you don't live with the person, and you provide that same regular, non-incidental assistance.
3. You're not a relative and don't live with the person, and you provide at least 80 hours of assistance a month.

Notice what the first two routes don't say. They don't set an hour count. An adult child who helps a parent regularly, whether under the same roof or across town, fits the words. The rule also says that in a home with more than one caregiver, more than one of you can qualify.

North Carolina's checklist asks it as a single question: "Are you a parent, guardian, caretaker relative or family caregiver of a person with a disability?" Answer yes to that, and the state's page says you do not need to meet the requirement. It adds, in the same breath, "You may have to provide documents to support your answer."

## What "Disability" Means Here, and Why It's Broader Than You Think

This is the sentence I'd underline. The rule defines "disabled individual" using the Americans with Disabilities Act definition at 28 CFR 35.108, and says the person "need not be eligible for Medicaid or other Federal programs on the basis of a disability." Your parent doesn't need a Social Security disability finding. They don't need to be on Medicaid at all.

CMS then wrote, in the preamble to the rule, that the term "could, therefore, include an older adult who requires assistance of varying scope in performing activities of daily living (ADLs) (bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, etc.) or other activities that keep older adults living at home and participating in community life." CMS said it encourages states to make their materials clear enough that "individuals providing supports to older adults understand that they could qualify."

Two cautions, because accuracy matters more than comfort here. First, CMS wrote "could," not "will." Your county DSS decides whether your parent's situation meets the definition, and North Carolina says it may ask for documents. Second, a parent who's sharp, mobile and simply enjoys your Sunday visits is not a disabled individual under this rule. The assistance has to be real and regular.

## If You Don't Fit the Exemption, Your Hours Still Count

Suppose your situation is thinner. Maybe your parent needs less help than the definition requires, or the county says no. You're not out of options.

The rule defines "work" to include "unpaid work (other than community service)." CMS says directly in the preamble that "the unpaid work of a family caregiver" who does not qualify for the exclusion "can also qualify as unpaid work" toward the 80 hours. North Carolina's page lists "some kinds of unpaid work" among the approved activities. Keep a log of the hours anyway. It costs you nothing and it may be the thing that saves the coverage.

The $580 route is the other door. Per NC Medicaid, the $580 can include money from other people in your household, such as a spouse, and some income that's not from a job, like unemployment benefits, counts too.

## The Money Trap Behind the Letter

Here's the consequence that almost nobody mentions, and it's the one that turns a caregiving problem into a housing problem.

If you lose Medicaid for not meeting this requirement, you can't just slide over to a subsidized marketplace plan. CMS explains in the rule that under section 1902(xx)(7)(B) of the Social Security Act, a person who would have been eligible for Medicaid "but for their failure to meet the community engagement requirement" is treated as having coverage available, and is "precluded from eligibility" for the premium tax credits that make marketplace plans affordable. The rule even requires the state's noncompliance notice to spell that out.

So the caregiver who loses coverage in March is uninsured in April. And I know exactly what happens next, because I used to be the person who showed up for it. The family looks around for the one asset that can fix a problem that size, and it's the house your parent is still living in. The "we buy houses" letter that's been sitting on the counter starts to look reasonable. Somebody calls an agent, and the agent, doing the job, wants it listed by the weekend.

You don't have to be the one who tells anybody no. That's the whole reason I do this the way I do it. Protecting the caregiver's coverage is the cheapest way I know to protect the parent's house.

## Six Things to Do Before the First Letter

### 1. Find out whether you're even in the group

Open NC Medicaid's checklist and read the whole list. If you're under 19, over 64, on Medicare, pregnant or recently pregnant, getting SSI or SSDI, a veteran rated 100 percent, or already determined disabled by NC Medicaid, the requirement doesn't apply to you. If none of those fit and you're a caregiver, the next step is the one that matters.

### 2. Tell your county DSS you're a family caregiver, now

North Carolina's page says it plainly: "You should tell your local DSS about your situation." Don't wait for January. Call or visit, say you're a family caregiver of a person with a disability, and ask what they want from you to confirm it. If they're not sure, ask them to write down that you asked. The state's advice when you're unsure is "Call your local DSS to ask about your situation. Do not guess."

### 3. Build the proof folder

NC Medicaid says to "collect any papers, medical records, official documents or other proof that you fall into one of these categories." For a caregiver of a parent, that usually means a letter from the parent's doctor describing what they can't do on their own, any care plan or home health assessment, proof of your relationship, and proof of where each of you lives. If you don't live together, a short written log of what you do each week and how often is worth keeping. Date everything.

### 4. Fix your address and get an enhanced ePASS account

Every page the state has published on this starts the same way: make sure your county DSS has your current address and phone number, because they will write to you, and "if your address is wrong, you could lose your Medicaid without knowing why." Then get an enhanced ePASS account. That's the only way to upload documents online.

### 5. Count your hours and dollars anyway

Even if you expect the exemption, start a simple monthly record in November: hours worked, hours of caregiving, any pay stubs, and household income. Per NC Medicaid, you'll need to meet the requirement for 3 of the 6 months before each renewal if you're not excluded. If the exemption comes through, you've lost nothing. If it doesn't, you've got the proof already.

### 6. Answer every letter the day it comes

The CMS rule gives you 30 calendar days after a notice of noncompliance to show you complied or that you're excluded. North Carolina says missing a deadline "is a common reason people lose their Medicaid," and that you usually have 60 days from the date on a termination letter to appeal. If a letter doesn't make sense, call the NC Medicaid Ombudsman at 1-877-201-3750 before the deadline, not after.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does caring for my aging parent exempt me from the NC Medicaid work requirement?

It can. The federal rule at 42 CFR 435.554 excludes a parent, guardian, caretaker relative or family caregiver of a person with a disability, and NC Medicaid's checklist asks that exact question. You qualify as a family caregiver if you live with the person and help regularly, or if you're a relative who helps regularly without living there. Your county DSS confirms it, and North Carolina says you may have to provide documents.

### Does my parent have to be on Medicaid or officially disabled for me to count as a family caregiver?

No. The rule defines a disabled individual using the ADA definition at 28 CFR 35.108 and states the person "need not be eligible for Medicaid or other Federal programs on the basis of a disability." CMS wrote that the term could include an older adult who needs help with activities of daily living such as bathing, dressing, toileting or eating. The county still decides whether your parent's situation fits, so bring a doctor's letter describing what they can't do alone.

### Do I have to live with my parent to get the caregiver exemption?

No. The rule gives a separate route for a relative who doesn't live with the person. If you're the son, daughter, stepchild, grandchild or sibling and you provide assistance on a regular basis that isn't solely incidental, you fit the words without an hour count. A non-relative who doesn't live with the person needs at least 80 hours of assistance a month.

### Does the Medicaid work requirement apply to my 70-year-old parent?

No. Per CMS, the requirement applies to adults between the ages of 19 and 64 who aren't on Medicare and who have Medicaid through the adult expansion group. NC Medicaid's checklist excludes anyone older than 64, anyone who qualifies for Medicare, and anyone the state has already determined to have a disability. A parent on Medicaid for nursing home or in-home care keeps that coverage the way it is today.

### What happens if I lose NC Medicaid for not meeting the work requirement?

You'll get a letter explaining why and how to appeal, and NC Medicaid says you usually have 60 days from the date on it. You can reapply at any time, but per NC Medicaid you must then meet the requirement for 3 months in a row before you apply. The federal rule also says someone who loses Medicaid this way is precluded from the premium tax credits that lower marketplace premiums, so the subsidized backup plan isn't there. Raise the caregiver exemption before it gets to that point.
