You've got about three days before the phone starts ringing at your parent's house, and the call is going to sound like good news.

On October 3, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services published a fact sheet announcing a one-time $90 rebate on Part B premiums. It's real money, and most people who qualify will see it in their bank account on or around October 8. Here's the part that matters for your family: it's automatic. There's no form, no application, no phone call to make. Nobody needs your mom's Medicare number to "release" it, "verify" it, or "speed it up."

Which means every call, text, or email this month that asks for her number to get her the $90 is somebody trying to take something much bigger than $90.

You know how this goes. The real thing hits the news. Your parent half-hears about it on the TV in the kitchen. Then a friendly voice calls and says her rebate is on hold, and all they need is a quick confirmation of her Medicare number. She's not careless. She's polite, she's heard the news is true, and the caller sounds like he works there.

I spent more than eight years on the buying side of houses, sitting at kitchen tables with families in transition. I'm not proud of all of it, but it taught me exactly how urgency gets used on older homeowners: take something real, attach a deadline, and make saying yes feel like the safe move. Scammers didn't invent that playbook. They just run it faster. I don't sell Medicare plans and I'm not giving plan advice here. This is the scam-safety habit, and it's one you can hand your parent tonight.

## What CMS Actually Announced

The source is a CMS fact sheet titled "Medicare Improvement Fund Premium Rebate Frequently Asked Questions," published October 3, 2026. Here's what it says, in plain English.

**The amount.** A single $90 rebate toward Part B premiums, paid in October 2026. One payment, not monthly. CMS describes it as a payment to offset the October premium.

**Who qualifies.** According to CMS, about 20.8 million people in Original Medicare Part B who meet three conditions: they live in the United States, they aren't receiving premium help from Medicaid, and they don't pay an Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (the income surcharge Medicare calls IRMAA).

**Who doesn't.** CMS states plainly that people enrolled in Medicare Advantage are not eligible.

**How it arrives.** Per CMS, the Social Security Administration handles most of the direct deposits, which most people should see on or around October 8. A follow-up email or letter is expected in mid-October. People without direct deposit get a paper check from the U.S. Department of the Treasury later in October, mailed to the address Medicare has on file. CMS says the check carries the message "Medicare Improvement Fund Payment; $90 Payment to Offset October Premium."

**Where to ask.** CMS directs eligibility questions to 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227). Starting October 15, 2026, payment status questions go to Social Security at 1-800-772-1213.

**Where the money comes from.** CMS says the Medicare Improvement Fund was created by Congress in 2008 to fund improvements to Original Medicare Parts A and B, and that Congress has drawn on it roughly 20 times since to offset other spending.

For context, CMS set the standard 2026 Part B premium at $202.90 a month, with a $283 annual deductible. So $90 covers a little under half of one month's standard premium. It's welcome. It isn't life changing. And it's exactly the size of number that makes a scam call feel believable, because nobody's suspicious of a modest, official-sounding amount that matches what they saw on the news.

Notice what's missing from the fact sheet: any step your parent has to take. That's not an oversight. It's the whole defense.

## What This Means for Your Family

### "Nothing to claim" is the sentence that protects her

Scams work in the gap between what's real and what a person thinks they have to do about it. This one has almost no gap, if your parent knows one fact. The rebate is automatic. There's nothing to claim.

Medicare.gov says Medicare will never call you to sell you anything or visit you at your home, and it tells people not to give their Medicare number to anyone except their doctor or people they know should have it. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance goes a step further: Medicare will only ask you to verify information if you contacted them first, and caller ID can be faked to show Medicare's name or number.

Put those together and you've got a rule your mom can follow without remembering a single detail of the rebate: if someone called her, she doesn't give them anything. She hangs up and calls 1-800-MEDICARE herself.

### Why a $90 call is after a lot more than $90

Your parent's Medicare number isn't a password to $90. It's a key to her medical identity. The FTC warns about callers who promise a "Medicare-approved" back or knee brace if she'll just read them her number, and it treats someone using her information to get medical care or services as identity theft. The FTC puts losses to Medicare from fraud, errors, and abuse at roughly $60 billion a year.

And a caller who gets her Medicare number on Tuesday has a reason to call back Thursday asking for a bank account number "for the deposit." The rebate gives them a cover story for both.

### The Medicare Advantage tell

Here's the part almost nobody is talking about, and it's the one that can protect your parent best.

People in Medicare Advantage don't get the $90 at all. So if your mom is in a Medicare Advantage plan and somebody calls about "her rebate," that call is fake on its face. No judgment call, no "but he sounded official." It's simply not something she's owed.

That only helps if somebody in the family knows which kind of Medicare she has. A lot of families don't. Medicare.gov's own guidance on the card is a good clue: people in a Medicare Advantage plan use the plan's card to get services, not their Medicare card. If the card she shows at the doctor's office has an insurance company's name on it, she's very likely in a plan. If she hands over the Medicare card itself, she's likely in Original Medicare, often with a supplement.

### Who else won't see a deposit

Two other groups are left out, and both are worth knowing so nobody panics on October 9.

If your parent gets help from the state paying her Part B premium, she's not on the list. Medicare.gov explains that Medicare Savings Programs and Medicaid can pay the Part B premium for people who qualify, and the CMS fact sheet excludes anyone already getting that premium help from Medicaid. That makes sense, since she isn't paying the premium in the first place.

If she pays the income surcharge, she's out too. CMS says the 2026 surcharge applies to individuals with modified adjusted gross income above $109,000, or $218,000 for joint filers, and affects roughly 8 percent of people with Part B.

So if no deposit shows up, that isn't a sign something went wrong, and it definitely isn't a reason to call back the number that texted her. It may just mean she's in one of these groups, or she's getting a paper check later in October.

### Real money plus a real letter equals a perfect cover

Look at the calendar. Deposits around October 8. A follow-up email or letter in mid-October. Medicare Open Enrollment runs October 15 through December 7, when Medicare.gov says people can switch between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage or change drug plans, with changes starting January 1.

That's three real things landing in the same few weeks. The FTC warns each open enrollment season that scammers impersonate Medicare during open enrollment, saying they sound professional and may even have some personal information already. This year they've got a fresh, true headline to hang it on.

The weeks when a real notice is expected are the weeks a fake one works best. Your job isn't to make your parent suspicious of everything. It's to give her one rule that holds no matter what the caller says.

## What To Do This Week

### Step 1: Tell her tonight, in one sentence

Don't send an article. Call her and say it out loud: "The $90 Medicare rebate is automatic. Nobody needs your Medicare number for it. If anybody calls about it, hang up and call me." Short enough to remember, specific enough to act on. If you've got siblings, send them the same sentence so she hears it twice.

### Step 2: Find out which kind of Medicare she has

Ask her to read you what's on the card she uses at the doctor's office. An insurance company's name usually means a Medicare Advantage plan, which means no rebate, which means any "rebate" call is fake. While you're at it, write down which plan she's in. You'll want it for open enrollment anyway, and you'll want it the day a hospital asks.

### Step 3: Check the bank, not the phone

If she's in Original Medicare, the place to confirm the $90 is her bank account around October 8, or her mailbox later in October if she doesn't use direct deposit. Not a text message, not a link, not a callback number someone else provided. After October 15, if it still hasn't shown up and she thinks she qualifies, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213 or Medicare at 1-800-633-4227, numbers you looked up yourself.

### Step 4: Practice the hang-up

This sounds silly until you watch a polite 80-year-old try to end a call with someone who won't stop talking. Give her permission out loud: "You can hang up mid-sentence. It isn't rude. It's the rule." Some families keep a sticky note by the phone that says "Medicare doesn't call. Hang up. Call 1-800-MEDICARE." Whatever works in her house.

### Step 5: Know where to report it

If she already gave out her number, don't let embarrassment slow you down. The FTC says to call 1-800-MEDICARE or your local Senior Medicare Patrol at 1-877-808-2468, and to report fraud at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If someone used her information to get care, report it at IdentityTheft.gov. Then start reading her Medicare statements with her each month. The FTC's advice is simple: if something on the statement doesn't match, ask the provider or plan to explain it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to apply for the $90 Medicare rebate?

No. According to the CMS fact sheet published October 3, 2026, the rebate is paid automatically. Most eligible people get a direct deposit on or around October 8, and others get a paper check from the U.S. Treasury later in October. Anyone asking for a Medicare number to release it is running a scam.

### Who is eligible for the $90 Part B rebate?

CMS says about 20.8 million people in Original Medicare Part B qualify if they live in the United States, aren't getting Part B premium help from Medicaid, and don't pay the income-related surcharge known as IRMAA. People enrolled in Medicare Advantage are not eligible.

### Does Medicare Advantage get the $90 rebate?

No. CMS states that beneficiaries enrolled in Medicare Advantage are not eligible. If someone in a Medicare Advantage plan gets a call or text about a $90 Medicare rebate, that alone is a strong sign it's a scam, and the safest move is to hang up and call 1-800-MEDICARE.

### What should I do if my parent didn't get the $90 rebate?

First check whether she's in a group that's excluded: Medicare Advantage, Medicaid premium help, or the IRMAA income surcharge. Some people also get a paper check later in October instead of a deposit. Starting October 15, 2026, CMS says payment status questions go to Social Security at 1-800-772-1213.

### Will Medicare call about the rebate?

Medicare.gov says Medicare will never call to sell you anything, and the FTC says Medicare will only ask you to verify information if you contacted them first. Caller ID can be faked, so treat any unexpected call about the rebate as a scam and call 1-800-MEDICARE yourself.
