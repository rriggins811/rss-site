You know how this conversation usually starts. Mom's at the kitchen table, the stack of mail is getting harder for her to handle, and she says it like she's been thinking about it for a while: "I just want to put the house in your name so you don't have to deal with all that later." It sounds loving. It sounds simple. And it's one of the most expensive sentences a family can say out loud without a professional in the room.

I spent eight years buying and flipping houses, a lot of them from families in the middle of exactly this kind of moment. I wasn't the one giving tax advice back then, and nobody else at those closings was either. What I saw, over and over, was the bill showing up after the paperwork was done: a deed signed years earlier "to keep things simple," and then a capital gain nobody saw coming when the house finally sold.

So this post isn't here to tell you no. Sometimes a transfer is the right answer. It's here so that you and your parent make that decision with the numbers in front of you, not in a hurry, and not because a deed felt like the responsible thing to do on a Tuesday afternoon.

Here's what you're actually deciding. When a house moves from your parent to you during their life, three different sets of rules wake up at once: federal income tax rules about basis, Medicaid rules about gifts, and, if your parent lives in North Carolina, the state's property tax rules for older homeowners. None of them are hard to understand once they're laid out. They're just never laid out at the kitchen table.

Let's lay them out, then walk through what to do this week.

## What the rules actually say

Start with basis, because it's where the biggest money hides. Basis is, roughly, what the IRS treats as the cost of the house when it's sold. The gain is the sale price minus the basis.

According to IRS Publication 551, Basis of Assets, when you receive property as a gift, your basis for figuring gain is the same as the donor's adjusted basis. In plain English, you take over your parent's old number. If they bought the house decades ago, that number is small. Publication 523, Selling Your Home, says the same thing for a home received as a gift: as a general rule, you use the donor's adjusted basis at the time of the gift.

Inherited property works differently. Publication 551 says the basis of inherited property is generally the fair market value on the date of the individual's death. Publication 523 says the same for an inherited home. That's what people mean by a "step-up." The decades of growth while your parent owned it generally don't get taxed when you sell soon after.

Then there's the home sale exclusion. Publication 523 says a seller may exclude the first $250,000 of gain from the sale of their home, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, if they owned it and lived in it for at least 24 months out of the 5 years before the sale. Your parent may qualify for that on their own house. You almost certainly won't qualify for it on theirs, because you don't live there.

Next, the gift itself. The IRS lists the 2026 annual gift exclusion at $19,000 per person receiving the gift. A house is worth more than that, so the IRS instructions for Form 709 say a gift tax return generally has to be filed when someone gives more than the annual amount to one person in a year. The return is due by April 15 of the year after the gift. The same instructions say any available applicable credit has to be applied against gift tax, and the IRS puts the 2026 basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000. For most families, that means the return is required paperwork, not a check. It still has to be filed.

Then Medicaid. Federal law, 42 U.S.C. 1396p(c), sets a 60-month look-back for assets given away for less than fair market value. A transfer inside that window can make a person ineligible for Medicaid coverage of nursing facility care for a period of time, and the statute sets the length of that period by dividing the value given away by the average monthly private-pay cost of nursing home care in the state.

Last, North Carolina property tax. Under G.S. 105-277.1, the state's Elderly or Disabled Homestead Exclusion takes the greater of $25,000 or 50 percent of the home's appraised value off the tax bill for a qualifying owner who's at least 65 or totally and permanently disabled and under the income limit. The NC Department of Revenue set that income limit at $38,800 for the 2026 tax year. The key word is owner.

## What this means for your family

### The same house, three very different tax results

Here's an illustration, not anyone's real file. Say your parents bought their house in 1988 for $80,000, and it's worth about $330,000 today. Ignore improvements and selling costs to keep the math clean.

**If your parent sells it while living there**, the gain is about $250,000, and if they pass the 2-of-5-year test, up to $250,000 of it can be excluded under Publication 523. The taxable gain can land near zero.

**If your parent deeds it to you now and you sell it next year for $330,000**, your basis is their $80,000. You've got about $250,000 of gain, and you don't get the home sale exclusion because it was never your main home. That gain is taxable to you.

**If you inherit it and sell it soon after for about what it was worth at death**, your basis is roughly $330,000, and the taxable gain can land near zero.

Same house. Same family. Same sale price. The only thing that changed was how and when the title moved. That's why "put it in my name so it's simple" deserves a second look. It can trade two tax-friendly paths for the one that isn't.

### The Medicaid clock starts the day the deed is recorded

The second cost is time. If there's any chance your parent will need nursing home care in the next five years, a gift of the house sits inside that 60-month look-back. That doesn't mean it's automatically a problem. It means the county will ask about it, and if it counts as a transfer for less than fair market value, your parent could face a stretch where Medicaid won't pay for the nursing home.

There are real exceptions in the federal statute, and they're worth knowing because families qualify for them more often than you'd think. Under 42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(2)(A), a home can be transferred without that penalty to a spouse, to a child under 21 or a child who's blind or disabled, to a sibling who has an equity interest in the home and lived there at least a year before your parent entered a facility, or to a son or daughter who lived in the home for at least two years before your parent entered a facility and provided care that let them stay home. If you moved in two years ago to take care of Mom, that last one might describe you. It's still a question for an elder law attorney, because the state decides whether the care qualifies.

Keeping the house in your parent's name isn't risk-free either. Federal law requires states to seek recovery from the estates of Medicaid recipients 55 and older for long-term care costs. If that's where your family is headed, read how North Carolina's estate recovery works before you choose a path. There isn't a no-tradeoff answer here. There's only the one that fits your family's facts.

### Your parent can lose a property tax break they're counting on

The third cost is quieter. If your parent gets North Carolina's Elderly or Disabled Homestead Exclusion today, it's because they're the owner. The statute defines an owner as a person holding legal or equitable title, and it counts a life estate as ownership. A plain deed to you can mean your parent isn't the owner anymore, and the exclusion can go away with it. On a house appraised at $200,000, half the appraised value is $100,000 off the taxable value, so that's not a small line on the bill.

### Your house becomes your problem, too

Once the house is in your name, it's your asset. It's exposed to your life, not just your parent's. That's not a statute, it's just how ownership works, and it's worth a plain conversation with an attorney before anyone signs.

## What to do before anyone signs a deed

### 1. Ask your parent what they're actually worried about

Most of the time, "put it in your name" isn't really about the deed. It's about a fear: who'll pay the bills if I get sick, who'll handle the house if I can't, who'll keep the family from fighting. Write down the real worry in one sentence. You'll be surprised how often the fix isn't a transfer at all. A durable power of attorney, for example, lets you act on your parent's behalf without taking title to the house.

### 2. Find the original basis

Look for the closing statement from when your parents bought the house, plus receipts for major improvements like a new roof, an addition, or an HVAC system. Put them in one folder. Every option above depends on this number, and it's much easier to find now than after a move.

### 3. Get the current value in writing

You don't need to list anything to learn what the house is worth. A local agent can give you a market analysis, or you can start with your county's tax value and know it's often behind the market. You need a real number to compare the three paths side by side.

### 4. Book one meeting with an elder law attorney and a CPA

Bring the basis folder, the value, your parent's income, and an honest read on their health. Ask three questions in writing: what each option does to income tax, what it does to Medicaid eligibility and estate recovery, and what it does to the homestead exclusion. There are other ways to set this up, including life estates and trusts, and the tax and Medicaid results differ. That's their expertise, not yours or mine.

### 5. Decide, then write down why

Whatever your family chooses, put the reason on one page with the date and the numbers you used. If a sibling asks in three years why the house went the way it did, you'll have the answer, and you won't be defending a memory.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What happens if my parent puts their house in my name?

You become the legal owner, and IRS Publication 551 says your basis for figuring gain is generally your parent's old basis, not today's value. If you later sell a house you never lived in, the gain can be taxable to you. The gift can also fall inside Medicaid's 60-month look-back and can affect North Carolina's homestead exclusion.

### Is it better to inherit a house or receive it as a gift?

For income tax, inheriting is often better. Publication 551 says inherited property generally takes a basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death, while gifted property generally keeps the donor's basis. Medicaid planning, estate recovery, and family goals can change the answer, so run both paths with a CPA and an elder law attorney.

### Do we have to file a gift tax return if Mom gives me the house?

Generally, yes. The IRS instructions for Form 709 say a return is generally required when someone gives one person more than the annual exclusion in a year, which the IRS lists as $19,000 for 2026. The return is due by April 15 of the following year. Because the applicable credit is applied first, most families filing for a house won't owe gift tax.

### Can my parent give me the house without hurting Medicaid eligibility?

Sometimes. Under 42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(2)(A), a home can go to a spouse, a child under 21 or a blind or disabled child, a qualifying sibling, or a son or daughter who lived there at least two years before the parent entered a facility and provided care that kept them home. Outside those exceptions, a gift inside the 60-month look-back can trigger a penalty period.

### Will my parent lose the NC senior property tax exclusion if they deed me the house?

They can. Under G.S. 105-277.1, the Elderly or Disabled Homestead Exclusion goes to a qualifying owner, and the statute counts a life estate as ownership. A plain deed that leaves your parent with no ownership interest can end it. The NC Department of Revenue set the income limit at $38,800 for the 2026 tax year.
