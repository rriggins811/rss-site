## What We Discussed

Jeff Armstrong gave me room to say the uncomfortable part out loud, and most hosts steer around it. For eight years I wrote the letters that show up in an older homeowner's mailbox offering cash, fast, as is. I know how those numbers get built, because I built them. This conversation is what I wish somebody had told the families I bought from.

Three things came out of it that matter to any family looking at a parent's house.

**1. A cash offer is not a price. It is a calculation about you.**

The number on that letter is not what your parent's house is worth. It is what the house is worth minus repairs, minus the buyer's margin, minus a discount for how quickly you seem to need this to be over. That last part is the piece nobody says out loud. Days on market, an out of state adult child, a parent already moved into care, all of it reads from the outside as leverage. The offer is priced against your exhaustion, not against the property.

**2. The five year look back turns a cheap sale into a benefits problem.**

This is the part that surprises families most. Selling a home at fair market value is a conversion. Cash replaces the house and nothing is penalized. But selling it well below fair market value, to a relative or to whoever mailed the most convincing letter, can be treated as a partial gift. That creates a penalty period where Medicaid will not pay at all, right when a parent needs it.

So a lowball offer is not simply money left on the table. It can disqualify a parent from the coverage the sale was meant to bridge to. If a family takes a cash offer, the price needs to be defensible as arm's length, with the value documented.

**3. There are seven or eight ways to sell a home. Most families are shown two.**

Traditional listing or a cash offer. That is the whole menu most people ever see. It leaves out selling as is on the open market, owner financing, a lease option, an investor sale run as a competitive process, and several others. Which one fits depends on the condition of the house, the real timeline, and what the money actually has to do. Sometimes the answer is that nobody should sell anything yet.
