## QUICK ANSWER

Yes. You can talk through a parent's house with a licensed real estate professional without signing anything or agreeing to sell. Most agents are paid only when a house sells, so the first conversation often drifts toward a listing. Ask up front: are you trying to list this house? I never take listings. I help families decide whether to sell, keep, or rent, who can legally sign, and how the money works for care, in a 30-minute call with no obligation. If your family does sell, I refer a vetted agent and am paid by that agent at closing, never by your family.

When I tell people what I do, the same thing happens almost every time. Someone says, "Wait. I can call you about my mom's house, and you won't try to list it?" Then they tell me the whole story, right there. At one senior expo this fall, about ten people did exactly that.

That reaction tells me something. A lot of families have a house question they have been sitting on for months, because every door they know of leads to a sales pitch.

## Why the first call usually turns into a pitch

It is not that agents are bad people. It is how they are paid. A listing agent earns a commission when the house sells, and nothing if it does not. So a conversation that starts with "we are just thinking" is, to them, the top of a sales funnel. Cash buyers are worse: their whole business is buying below value, so their first call is built to create urgency.

Neither one is set up to tell you, "Actually, keep the house for now."

## What a no-pressure conversation should cover

Before anyone talks about a price or a listing date, a family deserves answers to these:

- **Should the house sell at all?** Sometimes renting it pays for more months of care than selling. Sometimes keeping it protects it under Medicaid rules. Sometimes selling is right, but not yet.
- **How long does the money last?** Income plus savings plus what the house could bring, against the actual monthly cost of care, with yearly increases in the math.
- **Who can legally sign?** A power of attorney, a trust, a deed with two names, a parent who can no longer sign. This stops more sales than anything else, and it is cheaper to find out now.
- **Is there a reverse mortgage, a lien, or a listing already in place?** Each one changes the plan.
- **What happens to the stuff, and when?** The timeline for clearing a house drives almost everything else.

A good conversation ends with your family knowing its options, not with a signature.

## Questions to ask any agent before you share the details

1. "Are you hoping to list this house?" A straight answer is a good sign.
2. "Is there anything you'd want me to sign today?" The right answer is no.
3. "How are you paid if we decide not to sell?" Knowing this tells you whose interests the advice serves.
4. "Have you ever told a family not to sell?" Ask for an example.

In North Carolina, a licensed broker is also required to walk you through a short "Working with Real Estate Agents" disclosure at your first real conversation. It explains who the agent works for. Ask for it.

## How I do it

I am a licensed North Carolina broker (#361546, eXp Realty), and I spent eight years on the other side as a cash buyer before switching sides. Now I do one thing: help families think through a parent's house before anyone lists it.

- **The first step is a 30-minute call.** By phone, video, email, or text, whichever is easiest. No obligation.
- **I never take the listing and I never buy the house.**
- **If your family decides to sell,** I refer a vetted, senior-friendly agent anywhere in the country, and I am paid a referral fee by that agent from their commission at closing. It is disclosed in writing, your family pays nothing for it, and you are free to choose any agent you like.
- **If your family decides not to sell,** I am paid nothing, and that is fine. Sometimes keeping the house is the right answer.

If your parent's house is already listed with an agent, keep working with them. I will not get in the middle of that.

The best time is before anyone has signed anything: when a parent is starting to talk about moving, when a hospital stay has changed the timeline, or when an unexpected cash offer shows up in the mail.
